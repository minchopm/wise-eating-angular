A calorie count tells you
almost nothing about what you ate.

Two meals can land on the same number and do entirely different things to you. One carries iron, magnesium, B6 and fibre. The other carries sugar and a headache at four o'clock.

Wise Eating starts from the composition data instead — the same tables dietitians and food scientists work from — so you can see what a meal actually contains before you decide whether to eat it twice a week.

Natural language search

Ask for food the way you'd ask a person.

Type the constraint, not the keyword. The search reads quantities, comparisons, negations and diets, and filters {{ data.foods.toLocaleString('en-US') }} foods on the device — no round trip, no waiting.

Stack it with filters for allergens, diets, pH and alkalinity, and age suitability, and the columns you constrained stay visible in the results so you can see why something matched.

The data

American data. Global appetite.

{{ data.source }} is published by the United States Department of Agriculture, and it is the most complete public food composition database in existence. Canada leans on it. Researchers everywhere cite it. Most countries have nothing of the kind of their own.

That is why {{ site.name }} is not a US-only tool. Composition is a property of the food, not of the border it crossed — a lentil in Sofia and a lentil in Seattle are the same lentil. What changes is soil, storage and how you cooked it, and the app says so rather than hiding it.

Nutrient figures are estimates. Actual content varies with variety, soil, season, storage and preparation. For anything that matters clinically, use laboratory testing and professional advice.
